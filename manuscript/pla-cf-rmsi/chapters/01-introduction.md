# Introduction

Polymer parts that must carry load are already produced by fused filament fabrication, yet the bond between one deposited layer and the next still limits mechanical reliability. Omer and colleagues review that bond for fused deposition modelling, the material-extrusion route this article refers to as fused filament fabrication. They treat bond quality as a constraint that continues to govern both polymer parts and composite parts: a weak interface increases anisotropy, and actions taken before printing, during the build, or after the build each leave some of the constraint in place (Omer et al., 2025)<sup>1</sup>. Under that constraint, tension is only a partial description of the solid. A road may resist a pull directed along its length and still open when the load pries successive layers apart, so a ranking obtained in tension does not stand in for a second mode. Numerical prediction does not make the ranking portable. Enriconi and colleagues, reviewing models of fused filament fabrication, find structural reliability difficult to forecast once the filament contains fiber, because the filler can point in different directions and because adhesion from layer to layer changes the response a model has to match (Enriconi et al., 2025)<sup>2</sup>. A strength measured on one PLA printer, or on one short-carbon-fiber PLA printer, stays with that printer, that toolpath, and that test. The layout reported here records neither the width of the bond, nor the porosity of the part, nor the temperature history along a road. The interface limit, and the weak portability of predictions for fiber-filled filaments, are the reasons for judging one commercial short-carbon-fiber PLA in tension, compression, and flexure inside a single screen.

Layer thickness and raster angle earn a place in that screen because both have already changed measured strength, while the size of the change has to remain attached to the test that produced it. On fused-filament PLA, Gajjar and colleagues tracked tensile strength through four adjustments. Thinning the layers from 0.4 mm to 0.2 mm raised strength by as much as 12%. Moving the raster from 90° toward 0° raised it by as much as 40%. Heating the nozzle from 200 °C to 220 °C added up to 10%, and slowing the feed from 60 mm/s to 40 mm/s added up to 7% (Gajjar et al., 2025)<sup>3</sup>. All four of those gains belong to the PLA series at the levels named above. The present screen prints a short-carbon-fiber filament, uses another set of levels, and keeps three strength columns, so the percentages are not expected increments for these runs. Short-carbon-fiber PLA has its own layer-height evidence, and that evidence is tied to particular properties. Alkabbanie and colleagues associate a layer height of 0.1 mm with a 28.05% rise covering flexural strength and impact strength. In the same prints the fibers worsened the microstructure, increased crystallinity, and failed to move every mechanical result in one direction. A higher extrusion temperature raised impact strength and simultaneously lowered flexural strength (Alkabbanie et al., 2024)<sup>4</sup>. Layer height is a reasonable factor to vary once the filament contains short carbon fiber. A flexural or impact gain, the 28.05% figure included, is not a prediction of how tension or compression will move in the nine runs of this article.

Carbon fiber does not raise every strength, and the published direction of the change depends on the property, the fiber content, and the control that was printed. The contrasts that follow come from other filaments and other designs. The nine runs contain no neat-PLA control, so they support no statement that the commercial filament is stronger than neat PLA, and the external numbers are not a yardstick for those runs. In a honeycomb-infill series, Rogelj and colleagues stepped infill from 40% to 100%. At 40% infill the carbon-fiber PLA measured 17.75 MPa in tension, and at 100% infill it measured 28.72 MPa. Neat PLA at those same infill steps measured 30.75 MPa and 49.11 MPa, so the filled grade stayed below the neat polymer in that tensile comparison. Reducing layer height from 0.40 mm to 0.20 mm added 3.1% to the tensile strength of the filled grade (Rogelj et al., 2025)<sup>5</sup>. Changing composition splits tension from flexure more directly. Blake and colleagues increased the carbon-fiber fraction in PLA and observed tensile strength rising as flexural strength fell, with contents between the extremes presented as a compromise between the two responses (Blake et al., 2025)<sup>7</sup>. A matched flexural test can point the other way. In an ASTM three-point bend, Keshavamurthy and colleagues found an 88% flexural advantage for fused-deposition PLA containing 6 wt% short carbon fiber over unreinforced PLA printed under matched conditions, and their 3 wt% material also carried a larger load than the unreinforced polymer (Keshavamurthy et al., 2026)<sup>8</sup>. Raising the fiber content further can undo a gain when the fibers clump. Among the PLA composites made by Gök and Akay, tension was greatest at 17.47 MPa for the compound that contained 3% glass fiber, and a loading of 5% fiber lowered performance, an effect they attribute to agglomeration (Gök and Akay, 2025)<sup>9</sup>. Strength and toughness can part company as well. Alshihabi and colleagues describe PLA-CF as both stronger than neat PLA and more brittle. The ultimate tensile strength of 57.5 MPa in that paper is the figure given for PAHT-CF, so it does not describe PLA-CF (Alshihabi et al., 2025)<sup>10</sup>. Gök and Akay name lightweight durable parts as a possible use, and Alshihabi and colleagues present their comparison as guidance for parts that should be light, strong, and thermally efficient. That intended use still leaves the mechanical responses free to disagree with one another. No neat-PLA ranking is assigned to the filament printed here.

Infill density and raster orientation are the evidence that fixes the factors in the present screen. Mayandi and colleagues printed chopped carbon-fiber PLA in a design that also changed other settings, and the tensile strengths at 60%, 80%, and 100% infill were 25.9 MPa, 26.9 MPa, and 34.75 MPa. At those same densities the flexural strengths were 11.8 MPa, 12.55 MPa, and 18.4 MPa (Mayandi et al., 2024)<sup>11</sup>. Both modes responded to density. A comparison whose question is layer thickness, printing speed, and raster angle has to keep density at one value, or an infill shift will be mixed into the three factors under study. The L9 used here does so. Raster angle remains because, in PLA+, orientation has altered tension and flexure inside one experiment. Ramírez-Prieto and colleagues obtained both their highest tensile strength and their highest flexural strength from a 0°/0° raster. The 45°/−45° raster was their weakest pattern in flexure and the more ductile of the patterns they printed (Ramírez-Prieto et al., 2025)<sup>6</sup>. Raster angle is a credible factor when more than one mode is of interest. Compression is absent from that PLA+ experiment, so the compressive column in the present record has no source there. The paired 0°/0° instruction is also a different toolpath from the single 0° level that appears in this L9. The screen changes layer thickness, printing speed, and one raster angle, holds infill constant, and measures compression together with tension and flexure.

Even a campaign that already measures several mechanical responses, or that already fuses them into one grade, still selects a preferred setting according to which response is inspected and which rule performs the fusion. Verim and colleagues ran a Taguchi experiment on PLA with five specimens at each design point and recorded tension, compression, flexure, impact, hardness, and roughness. Infill density accounted for 55.21% of the tensile variation and for 80.86% of the compressive variation. In flexure that share was 25.66% and stood second in their ranking, while printing speed and raster angle placed below infill density and below wall thickness (Verim et al., 2025)<sup>12</sup>. Those shares describe PLA in a design that allowed infill to vary. They show that factor order is not common to every mechanical test, and that infill can dominate tension and compression when it is free. Because the present short-carbon-fiber layout holds infill fixed, it needs its own factor comparison and does not take over the PLA order. Disagreement among properties is visible inside an L9 before any grade is computed. Mohsein and colleagues treated tension, flexure, and Shore D hardness as joint outputs of an L9 on fused-deposition PLA. Layer thickness was the main factor in the two strength tests, infill was the main factor in hardness, and raster angle contributed little. On the Pareto front, tension preferred the low-infill side and flexure and hardness preferred the high-infill side. The authors note the statistical limits of their screening design and treat the flexure and hardness series as awaiting replication (Mohsein et al., 2026)<sup>13</sup>. An unreplicated L9 can display a conflict. It does not confirm a setting.

The selected point can move again once separate responses are fused by an explicit rule. For fused-deposition PLA, Patel and colleagues report three L9 preferences that do not match: tension was best at 70 mm/s with layers of 0.2 mm and infill of 40%; hardness was best at 60 mm/s with layers of 0.3 mm and infill of 40%; grey-relational aggregation was best at 70 mm/s with layers of 0.3 mm and infill of 40% (Patel et al., 2024)<sup>14</sup>. The aggregated grade kept a different layer thickness from the thickness preferred by tension. Grey relational analysis is a coherent way to construct that grade, and the grade can still select a different setting from any single response. The same dependence on the combining rule appears in carbon-fiber polyamide. Almuflih and colleagues built one grey relational grade for PA12-CF from surface roughness together with the tensile and flexural results. A later optimizer reported roughness of 4.63 μm, tensile strength of 88.5 MPa, and flexural strength of 103.12 MPa. Layer thickness, the infill used in that study, and build orientation each influenced the grade (Almuflih et al., 2024)<sup>15</sup>. The three optimized figures belong to PA12-CF under that procedure. They are not acceptance values for the short-carbon-fiber PLA runs. An index that gives the three modes equal weight, placed next to a score that keeps only the worst of the normalized modes, leaves this dependence open to inspection.

What is still missing is a score for short-carbon-fiber PLA that rescales tensile strength, compressive strength, and flexural strength by the minimum and the maximum of each column, averages the three rescaled values at equal weight, and is set beside a maximin score, all from one L9 that was not replicated. Mohsein and colleagues supply the replication caution that belongs with that design: their L9 could separate conflicting preferences in PLA, and they still class the screen as provisional until the tests are repeated (Mohsein et al., 2026)<sup>13</sup>. The material was PLA rather than short-carbon-fiber PLA, and hardness stood in the third column, so the caution is about what an unreplicated layout can decide. It is not a transfer of their property values onto PLA-CF. Weighting studies that do use carbon-fiber PLA are aimed at flexure rather than at the three strengths named above. Linul and colleagues optimized eight responses, every one of them flexural, on carbon-fiber PLA produced by additive manufacturing. In that flexural optimization, the dominant factors were printing temperature, layer thickness, and outer shells, while printing speed contributed less. Desirability was 0.9899 with GWO-MEREC-DFA weights, 0.9756 with GWO-CRITIC-DFA weights, and 0.9751 when the weights were equal (Linul et al., 2026)<sup>16</sup>. The equal-weight desirability lies near the other two and is not identical to them. Equal weight, on that result, is a convention the analyst writes down. It is not a balance read off the mechanics of the coupon, and the data behind it contain neither a tensile column nor a compressive column. The normalization has to be named for a separate reason. Comparing decision procedures on sustainability scores for an aircraft component, Malefaki and colleagues found that a change of method, a change of normalization, and a change of weights each shifted the combined index (Malefaki et al., 2025)<sup>17</sup>. The case is not a fused-filament data set. The comparison still requires any min-max score to be read only inside the observations that set its lower bounds, its upper bounds, and its weights. For the nine PLA-CF runs, the equal-weight score can order the runs by average relative standing, and the maximin score, equal to the smallest of the three normalized strengths, can order them by the mode in which they are relatively weakest. The two orders serve different screening questions. Neither order selects a setting for a service load, a geometry, or a filament outside the nine runs, and neither order converts the L9 from a screen into a confirmed process window.

This article performs that calculation for one commercial short-carbon-fiber PLA, Bambu Lab PLA-CF, with the nozzle at 220 °C and the bed at 55 °C. Infill density is kept at a single setting. Layer thickness, printing speed, and raster angle are varied in an unreplicated Taguchi L9. The thickness levels are 0.1 mm, 0.2 mm, and 0.3 mm. The speed levels are 30 mm/s, 45 mm/s, and 60 mm/s. The raster levels are 0°, 45°, and 90°, and each level is one angle rather than a pair of alternating angles. Every run contributes one tensile strength under ASTM D638, one compressive strength under ASTM D695, and one flexural strength under ASTM D790. Each of those cells contains a single observation. Inside a strength column, the lowest value is mapped to zero and the highest value to one by subtracting the column minimum and dividing by the column range. The Relative Multimode Strength Index is one hundred times the arithmetic mean of the three mapped values, with each value given the same weight. For any run, the maximin score retains whichever mapped value is the smallest of the three. The nine runs are ordered by the index and ordered again by the maximin score. After this scaling, a larger index is a better average position across the three modes, and a larger maximin score is a less severe weakest mode. Both scores are devices for screening prototype settings of lightweight parts, and both are confined to the nine runs that fix their minima, their maxima, and their equal weights. One observation in each cell provides no replicate error with which to test a difference among levels, and neither score qualifies a part for a duty the screen did not impose.
