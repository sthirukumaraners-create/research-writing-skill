# Discussion

A lightweight prototype printed in short-carbon-fiber PLA can be loaded in tension, compression, or flexure, and a screen intended for that use should keep both decision rules visible. Across the nine fused-filament runs, the equal-weight Relative Multimode Strength Index ranks Run 1 first, at 76.89. The tensile strength of 41.20 MPa and the flexural strength of 86.80 MPa are the maxima of their columns, so the normalized tensile and flexural scores are both 1.000, while the compressive strength of 53.20 MPa normalizes to 0.307. The maximin score retains that lowest normalized value and places Run 1 third when the question is the strongest worst mode. Run 2 leads the maximin ranking at 0.573 and stands second on the index at 71.08. The value 0.573 is its normalized tensile score, with normalized compression at 0.702 and normalized flexure at 0.857, so the three scaled responses sit nearer one another than the 1.000, 0.307, and 1.000 pattern of Run 1. Run 6 follows on the worst-case rule at 0.461, which is its flexural normalized score, with an index of 68.16 and the compressive maximum of 69.70 MPa. Table 4 and Figure 1 place these run-wise scores side by side. The index asks which coded setting is highest after the three scaled strengths are averaged. The maximin score asks which coded setting keeps the weakest scaled mode as high as possible. For a lightweight prototype that may see more than one of these load modes, the screen should report both ranks. Run 1 is the leading average inside this data set. That position is a screening result for these nine prints, and it supplies no structural qualification of the setting.

The layer-thickness averages give the index its largest descriptive spread, and the three strengths do not share that spread equally. At 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mm, the mean RMSI values are 60.55, 51.49, and 21.24, which is the pattern collected in Table 5 and Figure 2. Mean tensile strength follows the same descending order, 30.82, 23.73, and 20.47 MPa, and mean flexural strength falls from 73.63 MPa at 0.1 mm through 61.73 MPa at 0.2 mm to 42.10 MPa at 0.3 mm. Mean compressive strength breaks the order. It is 57.13 MPa at the thin layer, 62.53 MPa at 0.2 mm, and 52.33 MPa at 0.3 mm, so the middle layer leads compression while the thin layer leads tension, flexure, and the combined index. The tensile and flexural direction agrees with thinner-layer gains reported for PLA and for short-carbon-fiber PLA, and the agreement stops at direction. (Gajjar et al., 2025)<sup>3</sup> recorded a tensile increase of up to 12% on fused-filament PLA when layer thickness decreased from 0.4 to 0.2 mm. (Alkabbanie et al., 2024)<sup>4</sup> associated a 0.1 mm layer height with a 28.05% increase in flexural and impact strength of short-carbon-fiber PLA. (Rogelj et al., 2025)<sup>5</sup> reported a 3.1% tensile rise for PLA with carbon fiber when layer height fell from 0.40 to 0.20 mm under honeycomb infill. Those percentages were measured on other machines, at other thickness pairs, and with other infill choices, so they are not transferred to the Bambu Lab PLA-CF means. With infill held fixed, the thinner coded layer occupies the high end of the tensile, flexural, and RMSI averages, while compression peaks at the middle thickness.

Raster angle shifts tension and flexure and leaves compression almost unchanged. From 0° to 45° to 90°, mean tensile strength falls from 30.10 to 24.65 to 20.27 MPa, and mean flexural strength falls from 62.33 to 59.43 to 55.70 MPa. Mean compressive strength stays almost flat, at 56.90, 57.10, and 58.00 MPa. The label 0° is the raster code stored for that level of the array. The record does not establish that this code lay parallel to the tensile load or to the flexural span, so the decline from 0° to 90° remains a pattern in the coded factor. (Ramírez-Prieto et al., 2025)<sup>6</sup> found that a 0°/0° raster gave the highest tensile and flexural strength of PLA+, while a 45°/−45° raster gave the lowest flexural strength and more ductility. Their factor is a pair of raster directions on PLA+, and they did not report compression. Compression is the response that stays nearly level in the present means. (Gajjar et al., 2025)<sup>3</sup> associated a reduction in raster angle from 90° to 0° with a tensile gain of up to 40% in PLA. That percentage belongs to the PLA tensile experiment and is not carried into this PLA-CF screen. Printing speed occupies the middle of the RMSI ordering in this array. The speed means are 59.97 at 30 mm/s, 38.00 at 45 mm/s, and 35.30 at 60 mm/s, between the layer-thickness spread of 39.31 and the raster spread of 15.73. Nozzle and bed temperatures were held at 220 °C and 55 °C, so those speed means are not a temperature series. (Verim et al., 2025)<sup>12</sup> allowed infill density to vary in a Taguchi study of PLA and found infill contributions of 55.21% in tension and 80.86% in compression, with a flexural contribution of 25.66% that ranked second for that response. Printing speed and raster angle ranked below infill density and wall thickness. Verim's factor ranking cannot be inherited, because infill was free to vary in that design and was fixed here at a percentage the record does not contain. The order among layer thickness, speed, and raster describes this fixed-infill array only. (Linul et al., 2026)<sup>16</sup> also reported a smaller printing-speed effect than the effects of printing temperature, layer thickness, and outer shells on eight flexural responses of additively manufactured carbon-fiber PLA. Their factor list is a different experiment, and that smaller speed effect is not a variance share for the present runs.

Equal weight has a definite meaning only after the scaling that precedes it, and that meaning is equality on the normalized axis. Flexural strength across the nine runs spans 56.80 MPa, from 30.00 to 86.80 MPa. Compressive strength spans 23.80 MPa, from 45.90 to 69.70 MPa. Tensile strength spans 24.50 MPa, from 16.70 to 41.20 MPa. Min-max scaling maps each column onto the same 0–1 interval by using the observed minimum and maximum of that column. The 56.80 MPa flexural span and the 23.80 MPa compressive span therefore receive the same normalized length, as does the 24.50 MPa tensile span. Equal weight is equality after that scaling. Each normalized score contributes one third of the index, which gives the wider flexural column and the narrower compressive column the same vote. Inside the observed spans, one megapascal of flexure changes its normalized score by less than one megapascal of compression changes the compressive score, because both full spans are stretched onto one unit interval. The weight is consequently not a balance stated in newtons. Run 1 makes the arithmetic concrete: normalized tension and flexure are 1.000, normalized compression is 0.307, and the equal-weight mean still reaches 76.89 because two saturated scores offset the low compressive score. A different weight vector would move that mean. (Linul et al., 2026)<sup>16</sup> placed the same dependence on a numerical footing for flexural responses of additively manufactured carbon-fiber PLA. Equal-weight desirability was 0.9751, against 0.9899 for the GWO-MEREC-DFA search and 0.9756 for the GWO-CRITIC-DFA search. The gap between those desirabilities is small, and that small gap still shows that the weighting rule enters the composite. (Malefaki et al., 2025)<sup>17</sup> supply a methodological comparison on an aircraft-component sustainability data set, and that case is not an FFF study. On the sustainability data, both the multi-criteria method and the normalization technique changed the aggregated index, and weight changes moved the result as well. Their evidence can support a reading of normalization and weights as choices that reorder an index. That sustainability comparison cannot be read as an FFF parameter study. (Almuflih et al., 2024)<sup>15</sup> reach a compatible methodological point for carbon-fiber polyamide: a grey grade is a compromise whose identity depends on the aggregation, so the rule has to travel with the score.

Printed-polymer studies that already join several responses separate the preferred setting for one property from the preferred setting for the combination, which is the conflict a multimode screen has to keep visible. (Patel et al., 2024)<sup>14</sup> reported that separation for FDM PLA. The L9 tensile optimum was 70 mm/s, a 0.2 mm layer, and 40% infill. The hardness optimum was 60 mm/s, a 0.3 mm layer, and 40% infill. The combined grey-relational optimum was 70 mm/s, a 0.3 mm layer, and 40% infill. Speed in the grey-relational setting matches the tensile optimum, and layer thickness matches the hardness optimum, so the combined selection differs from each single-response selection. (Mohsein et al., 2026)<sup>13</sup> treated tensile strength, flexural strength, and Shore D hardness together on another L9 in FDM PLA. Layer thickness dominated tension and flexure, hardness followed infill, raster angle was weak, and the Pareto front separated a low-infill preference in tension from a high-infill preference in flexure and hardness. (Almuflih et al., 2024)<sup>15</sup> combined roughness, tensile strength, and flexural strength of PA12-CF by grey relational analysis, and a further optimizer reported 4.63 μm roughness, 88.5 MPa tensile strength, and 103.12 MPa flexural strength. That triple is the output of the combined search. The grey grade is the compromise among roughness, tension, and flexure, so the selected result belongs to the aggregation. The filament in that study is PA12-CF. Patel, Mohsein, and Almuflih thus place combined optima apart from single-response optima, on materials and response lists that differ from the tension, compression, and flexure recorded here. The Relative Multimode Strength Index is a simpler declared screen: an equal-weight mean of three min-max scores, reported beside the maximin score. The index was not benchmarked against grey relational analysis, and it was not benchmarked against a Pareto search. No comparison of that kind was run on these nine runs. The index remains a reporting rule for this PLA-CF array.

The nine runs leave the contribution of the chopped fiber unmeasured. Every specimen was printed from the same commercial PLA-CF filament, and a neat-PLA control was not included, so the screen claims no reinforcement benefit. External comparisons do not close that gap, because the papers disagree on which property improves when fiber is added. (Blake et al., 2025)<sup>7</sup> found that increasing the PLA-CF fraction raised tensile strength and lowered flexural strength, and they described mid-range fractions as a balance between those opposing movements. (Keshavamurthy et al., 2026)<sup>8</sup> reported a flexural gain on a matched print: FDM PLA with 6 wt% short carbon fiber had 88% higher flexural strength than unreinforced PLA produced under the same conditions, and the 3 wt% material also carried more load in an ASTM three-point bend. That comparison is confined to flexure. It assigns no tensile or compressive change to the fiber. (Gök and Akay, 2025)<sup>9</sup> reported a third pattern. Among the PLA composites they tested, the highest tensile strength was 17.47 MPa for PLA with 3% glass fiber, and a 5% fiber loading reduced performance, which they attribute to agglomeration. The comparison is indirect for a commercial chopped-carbon PLA filament, since the leading tensile material in that finding is a glass-fiber compound. The same finding indicates that a higher short-fiber loading can lower printed tensile strength. (Alshihabi et al., 2025)<sup>10</sup> found that PLA-CF improved strength relative to neat PLA and was more brittle. The 57.5 MPa ultimate tensile strength given in that paper belongs to PAHT-CF, so it is not a PLA-CF figure for the strengths measured here. Blake and colleagues report a flexural loss, and Keshavamurthy and colleagues report a flexural gain. Gök and Akay report a tensile penalty at the higher short-fiber loading. Alshihabi and colleagues report a strength gain joined to greater brittleness. The property that improves is not shared across the four papers. None of those contrasts was part of the present L9, and a neat-PLA arm would be required before any fiber benefit could be discussed for this filament.

The index stays inside the design that produced it. One observation occupies each cell. The sample standard deviations of 7.91 MPa in tension, 7.85 MPa in compression, and 17.34 MPa in flexure use the n−1 divisor across the nine runs, and they describe that cross-run scatter. They are not replicate standard errors, and the level means remain balanced averages of three runs. Those averages can describe the array. They cannot serve as a significance test. In a three-factor L9, two-factor interactions are not separable from the main-effect averages, so the ordering of layer thickness, speed, and raster angle still contains those interactions. Infill density was held constant, and the percentage was not recorded. Specimen dimensions were not recorded. No confirmation print was made. Run 1, Run 2, and Run 6 were not built again, and no confirmation strength is available to set beside the screening values. The index would also change if the data set changed. Each normalized score uses the minimum and the maximum observed in its own column, so a new row with a higher flexural strength, or a lower compressive strength, would move the endpoints and could reorder both the equal-weight ranks and the maximin ranks. (Mohsein et al., 2026)<sup>13</sup> have already called their unreplicated L9 mechanical screen provisional. Their abstract states the statistical limits of the screening design and calls the flexural and hardness campaigns provisional pending replication. The present tension, compression, and flexure screen shares that unreplicated limit. A replicated follow-up, with the infill percentage and the specimen geometry written into the record and with more than one specimen at each cell, is the measurement that can ask whether these ranks persist. Until that measurement is available, the two ranks remain a lightweight prototype screen of these nine PLA-CF runs.
