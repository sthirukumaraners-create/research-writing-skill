# 4. Characterization of Shape-Memory Polylactic Acid

## 4.1 Thermal transitions and dynamic mechanical analysis

The switching temperature is measured before anyone bends a hinge. Differential scanning calorimetry and dynamic mechanical analysis are the tools the abstracts name. Umayanga et al. (2026) tracked glass transition and activation temperature together as polyethylene glycol content rose. Robsan Abebe et al. (2026) report an endothermic peak of 9.09 J/g and an exothermic peak of 6.41 J/g for the polylactic acid grades they actuated, then used microscopy to see what that thermal cycle did to interlayers. Doostmohammadi et al. (2024) used both differential scanning calorimetry and dynamic mechanical thermal analysis to judge compatibility of polylactic acid with poly(methyl methacrylate) before trusting a recovery ratio. Liu et al. (2022) used dynamic mechanical analysis to show that graphene narrowed the glass-transition difference between polylactic acid and polybutylene adipate terephthalate in a monofilament. Kacem et al. (2025) include dynamic mechanical analysis in a broader thermo-mechanical suite on short yucca/polylactic acid, where a mechanically processed fiber raised tensile strength by 22% to 61 MPa and compressive strength by 20% to 89 MPa relative to a water-retted fiber. Bourgogne et al. (2026) used monotonic and dynamic mechanical tests on the filament itself.

These measurements justify a programming temperature. They do not, in the abstracts, supply the storage-modulus curves that a designer would digitize. A statement such as “carbon-fiber polylactic acid shows a storage modulus of a given value above the glass transition” would be an invention in this review. What can be said is that dynamic mechanical and calorimetric data are already being used to place blends and plasticized nanocrystal composites inside an activation window from about 40 °C to the 65–85 °C training band (Doostmohammadi et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Umayanga et al., 2026), and that extrusion and hydrothermal aging move the same transitions (Bourgogne et al., 2026; Bouguermouh et al., 2026; Fijoł & Mathew, 2023). Fijoł and Mathew (2023) are particularly relevant to any water-triggered or water-aged device: after three weeks in water, cellulose-nanofiber and chitin-nanofiber polylactic acid filters lost about 10 °C of glass-transition temperature, while neat polylactic acid was nearly unchanged for up to seven weeks. A transition measured on a dry filament is not the transition of a wet printed filter.

## 4.2 Recovery force, energy, and fatigue

Recovery ratio is the dominant reported outcome. Force and energy appear when the specimen is a lattice or a fatigue bar. Desole et al. (2026) publish the clearest force numbers: peak force and specific energy absorption over three load-and-recover cycles, with the heating method as the experimental factor. The drop from about 200 N to about 130 N in the stronger lattice is a cyclic-softening result, not a blocked-recovery stress measured at fixed length in a thermal chamber. Fereydoonpour et al. (2025) put fused-deposition polylactic acid and polylactic acid/thermoplastic polyurethane bi-materials through rotary-bending fatigue and then through a recovery step. A first attempt at 100 Hz delaminated the specimens. A vibration calculation showed the natural frequency sitting near 100 Hz, so the life tests were moved to 20 Hz. Temperature then had about 64% more influence than time on the shape-memory response, and fatigue loading was described as 137% more effective than monotonic loading. Adding 25% thermoplastic polyurethane increased the deformation rate by about 40% on average, and fatigue life fell as elastomer content rose. Fracture surfaces showed layer separation, cavities, and beach marks.

That sequence is a useful warning about test design. A recovery experiment that cyclically loads a layered beam near a resonant frequency measures delamination, not the intrinsic recovery of the polymer. Gharehbaghi et al. (2025) and Javadian et al. (2025) stay on the mechanical side of the same problem for continuous glass/polylactic acid honeycombs and rotating-bending bars, reporting residual stiffness protocols and a strong role for fiber angle and infill, without a recovery ratio. Shahar et al. (2022) trade impact resistance downward and fatigue life upward as kenaf particle content increases in a printed orthosis-motivated composite. The shape-memory literature and the fatigue literature are adjacent. They share interlayer failure as a mechanism (Fereydoonpour et al., 2025; Robsan Abebe et al., 2026; Vălean et al., 2024b). They do not yet share a specimen.

## 4.3 Free recovery, hot water, hot air, and the missing shared method

Free-recovery demonstrations in the set include a folded hinge that opens, a gripper that closes, a foam that returns, a lattice that is reloaded, and a hygromorph that curls in humid air (de Kergariou et al., 2023; Desole et al., 2026; Honari et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Umayanga et al., 2026). Desole et al. (2026) are the study that puts hot water and localized hot air on the same geometries. Hot air gave the more consistent cyclic energy absorption and avoided part of the hydrostatic damage of immersion. Bouguermouh et al. (2026) use hot water differently, as a 45 °C aging bath lasting long enough for flax composites to approach 20% uptake, and then they measure whether fixity and recovery survived. Water is therefore both a possible actuation medium and a degradation medium. A single “water-bath test” does not name which of those roles was intended.

No abstract in the screened set cites an ASTM or ISO standard as the method for macroscopic shape fixity or shape recovery of a printed part. Mechanical subsets do cite ordinary plastics standards where they apply: Vălean et al. (2025) use ISO 178 and ISO 179-1 for bending and impact of notched polylactic acid and short-carbon polylactic acid. Golan et al. (2026) and Wan Ishak et al. (2025) invoke biocompatibility and, in the latter, ISO 10993-5 for cytotoxicity of a scaffold, which answers a different question. Aghabarari et al. (2025) discuss characterization as one pillar of polylactic acid 4D printing and do not, in the abstract, name a consensus printed-part method. The practical consequence is visible in the numbers themselves. Fixity near 100% in a blend coupon (Song et al., 2023), recovery near 25% after 50 hinge cycles (Huang et al., 2025), and a force drop in a lattice (Desole et al., 2026) cannot be ranked on one scale. A useful standard would fix the programmed strain or fold angle, the constraint during cooling, the recovery temperature relative to the measured glass transition, the cycle count, and whether the fiber direction is along or across the strained section. Until that exists, authors should at least report those five conditions beside Rf and Rr. The studies that already do so—Huang et al. (2025), Hacıoğlu et al. (2026), Umayanga et al. (2026), and Desole et al. (2026)—are the ones from which a process window can be copied.
