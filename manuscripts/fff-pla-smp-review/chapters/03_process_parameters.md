# 3. Fused-Filament Process Parameters

The fused-filament variables that enter shape-memory papers are layer thickness, raster angle, printing speed, nozzle or printing temperature, and the temperatures of programming and recovery. Infill appears mainly in mechanical optimization of reinforced polylactic acid, and in the relative density of cellular recovery specimens. Treating these as one ranked list of “the” shape-memory parameters would flatten evidence that is still split by what each study measured.

## 3.1 Raster angle and build orientation

Raster is the in-plane direction of the deposited bead. It is also, in a continuous-fiber print, the direction of the reinforcement. Those are not the same experiment. Hamidi et al. (2026) included raster angle with printing speed, layer thickness, and printing temperature in an L9 design on polylactic acid/thermoplastic polyurethane. For fixity, raster angle ranked as the most influential of the four printing parameters and printing speed as the least. Analysis of variance then judged the printing parameters, as a group, to have minimal and statistically insignificant effects on fixity. The ranking and the significance test need to be kept together. Raster may be the least small of a set of small effects. Confirmation prints aimed at recovery ratio, not fixity, and the largest recovery-ratio gain they report is 3.975%. Doostmohammadi et al. (2024) also included raster angle, with poly(methyl methacrylate) content, nozzle temperature, and printing speed, in an optimization that reached 100% recovery and 65.2 MPa. The abstract does not isolate the raster level that the neural network preferred, so the result supports raster as a factor inside a successful window, not a preferred angle.

Where the fiber itself is the raster, the angle is no longer a small effect. de Kergariou et al. (2023) changed curvature by changing path and the tilt of passive flax layers, with the largest gain at 40°. Moradi et al. (2023) printed iron-filled polylactic acid at 0°, 45°, and 90° to an applied magnetic field while magnetizing during fused deposition. Strength rose 21.4% when a field was present and the bead ran at 0° along the tensile direction, and specific magnetization along the deposition direction rose 63.46% when the field was applied. Their optimum combined magnets under the bed with that 0° deposition. The angle here couples a magnetic axis to a mechanical axis. It is evidence that deposition direction can be a functional direction, and it is not a thermal shape-memory ratio.

Build orientation and planned laminates dominate the mechanical carbon-fiber papers. Toprak et al. (2026) compared flat, on-edge, and upright builds of polylactic acid and carbon-fiber polylactic acid at fixed process settings. Orientation changed energy consumption more than the choice of material, and the highest tensile strength for both materials was the on-edge build. Baharlou and Ma (2025), printing short-carbon and neat polylactic acid with a robotic pellet extruder, found that a quasi-isotropic layup improved the weak directions of both materials, while neat polylactic acid remained stronger in tension. Gajjar et al. (2026) selected 0° raster, among layer thickness, feed rate, nozzle temperature, and infill, for a multi-response optimum of short-carbon polylactic acid: 46.6 MPa tensile strength and 1390.5 MPa elastic modulus at 0.35 mm layers, 70 mm/s, 220 °C, and 100% infill. Selvamani et al. (2026) selected 45° and a 0.5 mm raster width, with 10 wt% New Zealand flax, for a modulus of 4453.85 MPa and a flexural stress of 73.395 MPa. The angles differ because the objectives differ. A 0° tensile coupon and a 45° flexural coupon are both rational. Neither angle has been shown, in this open set, to be the recovery direction of a carbon-fiber shape-memory part.

## 3.2 Layer thickness

Layer thickness changes the number of interfaces in a given height and the thermal history of each bead. Hamidi et al. (2026) ranked it as the most influential printing parameter for recovery ratio in polylactic acid/thermoplastic polyurethane, and printing temperature as the least. That is the most direct shape-memory statement available. It does not specify whether the better recovery sat at the thin or the thick end of their L9, and the confirmation gain in recovery ratio remained under four percentage points. Robsan Abebe et al. (2026) approached layer architecture through commercial filaments and a thermostat-valve geometry rather than through a declared layer-height series. Optical microscopy after differential-scanning-calorimetry cycling showed interlayer erosion, especially in two of the three polylactic acid grades, and scanning electron microscopy ranked the grades by porosity. The layer stack limited how the part could deform, and heat exposure damaged the interfaces that the stack depended on. A recovery of 6 mm in 2 s shows that a layered polylactic acid part can move quickly. It does not assign that speed to a 0.1 mm layer rather than a 0.3 mm layer.

Mechanical optima for layer height are material-specific and should not be borrowed as recovery optima. Vigneshwaran et al. (2024) obtained their highest wood-filled tensile and flexural strengths, both 16 MPa, at 0.20 mm, with 90% infill and a hexagonal pattern, inside a set that also included 0.15 mm and 0.30 mm. Jeevan et al. (2026) report 28.86 MPa for neat polylactic acid at 0.16 mm, 200 °C, and 90 mm/s, and 41 MPa for 0.5 wt% graphene at 0.18 mm, 220 °C, and 60 mm/s. Gajjar et al. (2026) landed on 0.35 mm for short-carbon polylactic acid when strength, modulus, roughness, and deposition efficiency were optimized together. Faidallah et al. (2024) varied face-sheet thickness of wood/polylactic acid honeycomb sandwiches, not the smeared layer height of a solid bar, and thicker faces raised bending resistance. Bermudo Gamboa et al. (2023) included layer thickness with temperature and speed as parameters that can move fatigue, dimensional accuracy, and geometric deviation of short-carbon polylactic acid, without a shape-memory endpoint. Thinner layers multiply interfaces. Whether that helps heat reach a switching segment faster, or simply multiplies the weak planes Robsan Abebe et al. (2026) saw erode, is not settled by a single recovery curve in this set.

## 3.3 Infill and relative density

A common design intuition is that a sparse infill lets hot air or hot water reach the core sooner, while a solid infill stores more recovery stress. The retrieved abstracts do not contain that paired experiment. What they contain is a mechanical preference for high infill when strength is the objective, and a cellular-architecture result when recovery under repeated load is the objective. Gajjar et al. (2026) included 100% infill in the parameter set that maximized the combined mechanical and efficiency response of short-carbon polylactic acid. Vigneshwaran et al. (2024) likewise associated 90% infill with the strongest wood-filled coupons among 30%, 60%, and 90%. Javadian et al. (2025) list infill density, with a +45/−45 fiber orientation, among the factors that matter for rotating-bending fatigue of continuous glass/polylactic acid at 16% fiber by volume. Those are survival and strength results.

Desole et al. (2026) come closest to the actuation question, and they change architecture and heating method rather than a percentage infill in a solid coupon. After screening seven lattices, they cycled Leaf_Spring and NS_Spring structures three times with hot water or localized hot air. Localized hot air raised specific energy absorption 9–11% relative to hot-water activation and reduced the geometric degradation they associate with hydrostatic effects. Leaf_Spring force fell from about 200–210 N on the first cycle to about 120–130 N on the third, while specific energy absorption started near 175 mJ/g. NS_Spring started near 60 N and lost less of its performance, about 32% against about 42%. They treat thermal-activation mode, architecture, and relative density as coupled variables. The result supports density as a recovery variable in cellular polylactic acid. It does not support a general claim that 20–50% infill actuates faster than 100% infill in a fiber-reinforced bar.

## 3.4 Speed, cooling, and crystallinity

Printing speed appears in the shape-memory optimizations as one factor among several. Huang et al. (2025) include 50 mm/s in the hinge setting that gave 99.53% first-cycle recovery, together with geometry and a 65 °C training temperature, so the speed is part of a package. Hamidi et al. (2026) rank printing speed as the least influential factor for fixity. Doostmohammadi et al. (2024) include it in a pellet-printing optimization without quoting the selected speed in the abstract. Speed therefore has a documented seat in successful windows and a weak unique effect where it was ranked against layer thickness and raster.

The thermal consequence of extrusion is clearer in filament studies than in recovery ratios. Bourgogne et al. (2026) compared 1.75 mm coil filament with the same grades after extrusion through a 0.4 mm path, for neat polylactic acid and for 20 wt% hemp, flax, and wood. Extrusion shifted the glass transition of every grade and lowered Young’s modulus by about 14% for neat polylactic acid and by up to nearly 50% for the hemp-reinforced grade. That is a process–structure result upstream of the printer: the act of melting and drawing the reinforced filament changes the stiffness the nozzle will later deposit. Tolcha et al. (2026) report a related thermal shift after enset and sisal fibers were compounded for fused deposition, with decomposition moving from 348 °C to 386 °C and melting point from 150 °C to 171 °C, which is a stability result rather than a crystallinity-to-recovery map. Liu et al. (2022) used dynamic mechanical analysis on graphene-modified polylactic acid/polybutylene adipate terephthalate monofilaments and report that added graphene reduced the glass-transition gap between the two polymers, with mechanical properties improved at 0.2 wt% graphene. None of these abstracts closes the loop from a faster nozzle, to a measured crystallinity, to a measured fixity. Huang et al. (2026) vary drawing speed from 15.7 to 22.0 mm/s and impregnation temperature from 225 °C to 235 °C for continuous-carbon/polylactic acid prepreg, and they quantify unimpregnated area and nominal fiber volume fraction. That speed controls impregnation quality. It is the right kind of variable for a later shape-memory study, and it is not itself a shape-memory result.

Taken together, the process evidence supports a cautious order of influence. Programming and training temperature move fixity, recovery time, and cycle life by large, repeated amounts (Hacıoğlu et al., 2026; Honari et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025). Blend ratio moves ductility and, depending on the metric, recovery (Fereydoonpour et al., 2025; Hamidi et al., 2026; Rahmatabadi et al., 2024). Layer thickness is the printing parameter most often implicated in recovery ratio, with a small confirmed gain in the one study that ranked it (Hamidi et al., 2026). Raster and deposition direction are decisive when they are the fiber or magnetic axis (de Kergariou et al., 2023; Moradi et al., 2023) and easy to over-interpret when they are one setting in an unfilled or blended coupon. Infill and nozzle speed are well mapped for strength and poorly mapped for actuation rate.
