# 2. Fundamentals of the Shape-Memory Effect in Printed Polylactic Acid

## 2.1 What is stored, and what switches

A shape-memory polymer holds a permanent shape with a network of constraints and a temporary shape with a phase, or a set of chains, that can be frozen and later released. Aghabarari et al. (2025) organize the molecular accounts used for polylactic acid and its blends into a dual-state mechanism, a dual-component mechanism, and a partial-transition mechanism. The open experimental papers rarely re-derive those pictures. They operationalize them. Crystals, entanglements, a co-continuous elastomer, or a continuous yarn are treated as the features that remember the printed geometry, and the mobile amorphous polylactic acid is treated as the feature that allows programming (Aghabarari et al., 2025; Song et al., 2023; Umayanga et al., 2026).

Song et al. (2023) make the switching-segment idea concrete in a blend. Lactic acid/caprolactone-modified chitosan was used to connect amorphous regions of polylactic acid and polycaprolactone. The addition that best compatibilized and toughened the blend, 0.5 g of modified chitosan in 10 g of blend, also gave the best printed shape-memory response, with fixity of 100% and a shape-memory ratio of 94.5%. Dantas et al. (2026) locate the permanent and functional network in morphology rather than in a crosslink chemistry. Carbon black stayed in the ethylene-glycidyl methacrylate phase of a co-continuous polylactic acid blend, which raised impact strength from 150 J/m to 450 J/m at 12 phr, lifted conductivity by about seven orders of magnitude to roughly 10−5 S/cm, and allowed both thermal and electrical recovery. The filler is doing two jobs: it dissipates impact, and it gives a path for Joule heating. It is not, in that abstract, described as a carbon fiber that orients with the nozzle.

Plasticizer content moves the temperature at which the amorphous phase lets go. In Umayanga et al. (2026), polyethylene glycol of higher molecular weight depressed the glass transition further than the lower-molecular-weight grade, from 49 °C toward 40.5 °C, and the activation temperature followed it from 49 °C to 40 °C. The cellulose nanocrystal content was held constant, so the thermal shift is a matrix effect. That matters for fiber design. A stiff nanocrystal or yarn does not set the switching temperature by itself if a mobile additive has already moved the glass transition.

## 2.2 The cycle the studies actually run

The programming cycle in this literature is a sequence of temperatures and constraints, not a single material constant. Hacıoğlu et al. (2026) state the controlling trio for 4D-printed polylactic acid as programming temperature, holding time, and recovery temperature, read out as recovery time, shape fixity ratio, and shape recovery ratio. Their Latin-square experiment, with machine-learning surrogates and a multi-objective search, placed a knee at 67.5 °C programming, 2.0 min of holding, and 80.0 °C recovery. The surrogates, checked by leave-one-condition-out validation, predicted 3.14 s, 99.72% fixity, and 91.70% recovery. Holding time, within the window they tested, did not add a significant further rise in fixity once programming temperature had moved from 50 °C to 55 °C and fixity had already climbed from 90.2% to 98.8%.

Honari et al. (2025) separate hot and cold programming in foamed polylactic acid. Hot programming improved shape fixity, and cold programming improved shape recovery. Higher azodicarbonamide content, which creates the in-situ foam, pushed fixity toward about 96% and reduced recovery. Huang et al. (2025) use “training temperature” in the same role as programming temperature for a folded hinge. The optimum they report—hinge thickness 0.8 mm, length 0.6 mm, printing speed 50 mm/s, training at 65 °C—recovered 99.53% on the first cycle. Training at 75–85 °C recovered faster and lasted for fewer cycles. The cycle is therefore a trade between how completely the temporary shape is locked and how quickly, and how often, it can be released.

Moisture is a different cycle with the same logic of a frozen and a released state. de Kergariou et al. (2023) did not heat continuous-flax/polylactic acid hygromorphs through a glass transition to recover a bend. They exposed a printed architecture to 98% relative humidity and let differential swelling, organized by the filament path, generate curvature. The permanent shape is still the printed path. The switch is water uptake in the fiber and the matrix, which Muthe et al. (2022) already flag as a second actuation mode alongside thermal shape memory of polylactic acid.

Electrical triggering adds a heating method rather than a new polymer mechanism. Dantas et al. (2026) report nearly complete fixity, about 100%, thermal recovery above 78%, and electrically triggered recovery around 90%. The current heats the carbon-black network. The chains still pass through a thermal switch.

## 2.3 Fixity and recovery as reported ratios

The studies report two percentages. Shape fixity is the share of the imposed deformation that remains after the part is unloaded in the cold, or otherwise locked, state. Shape recovery is the share of that fixed deformation that is regained when the switch is activated. Hamidi et al. (2026) name them Rr% and Rf% and treat them as the ordinary measures of polylactic acid/thermoplastic polyurethane performance. This review follows that usage. Individual papers do not reprint one common algebraic definition in their abstracts, so numerical comparisons below are comparisons of reported percentages, not a re-reduction of raw strains.

The percentages span almost the full scale, and the span is informative. Song et al. (2023) report 100% fixity and 94.5% shape-memory ratio for the best chitosan-compatibilized polylactic acid/polycaprolactone print. Doostmohammadi et al. (2024) report 100% shape recovery for an optimized polylactic acid/poly(methyl methacrylate) blend that also reached 65.2 MPa tensile strength. Umayanga et al. (2026) report about 93% fixity and about 94% recovery for a gripper at 40.5 °C, and about 98% retention of the original recovery ratio after fifteen cycles. Bouguermouh et al. (2026) report about 100% fixity and about 100% recovery for the unaged neat polylactic acid/polyethylene terephthalate glycol blend. After hot-water aging, fixity at 15 wt% flax was about 90%, and the highlighted recovery for that flax composite was about 97%. Huang et al. (2025) show that a first-cycle value near 100% is not a material constant: the same hinge family fell to about 25% recovery after 50 cycles at 65 °C. A high first-cycle ratio and a durable ratio are different properties.

Two further results warn against reading every percentage as the same experiment. Fereydoonpour et al. (2025) emphasize deformation rate and the relative influence of temperature, time, and fatigue loading in polylactic acid/thermoplastic polyurethane metamaterials, and they conclude that shape-memory response increased as thermoplastic polyurethane increased. Hamidi et al. (2026), measuring Rr% and Rf% across 0–50 wt% thermoplastic polyurethane, find the highest ratios in pure polylactic acid and a decline as elastomer content rises. The disagreement is useful. One study is tracking how much additional deformation a softer bi-material architecture can sustain under fatigue and still treat as recoverable. The other is tracking the classical ratios on blend specimens. Elastomer content can raise the deformation that is available and still lower the fraction of a programmed strain that is fixed and returned. Device design has to choose which quantity matters.

## 2.4 Fibers as constraints, and the elongation penalty

A continuous fiber or a well-bonded particle can act as a net-point because it does not flow when the amorphous matrix does. The clearest geometric evidence is the flax hygromorph. de Kergariou et al. (2023) varied the print path and the angle of passive layers. Cross-ply architectures with bent filaments produced curvature amplitudes of 1.9 × 10−3 mm−1 and 7.9 × 10−3 mm−1 depending on position. Tilting the passive-layer orientation to 40° increased actuation curvature by 0.0072 mm−1 relative to a 0° tilt. The fiber direction is the program. Rahmani et al. (2026) show the stiffness version of the same idea under heat. Continuous silk plus 3 wt% bamboo charcoal raised tensile strength to 108 MPa, a 213% increase over polylactic acid, raised bending strength by 247% over polylactic acid, cut the burning rate by 41%, and still allowed full shape recovery after 25% compression and 85% recovery after 45% compression once heat was applied. The fiber architecture stored load-bearing capacity and did not erase recovery at those compressive strains.

Short carbon fiber does not automatically join that class. In the mechanical studies, it often lowers strength and fatigue life even while it is present as a reinforcement on the label (Baharlou & Ma, 2025; Bermudo Gamboa et al., 2023; Vălean et al., 2024b, 2025). Those papers do not report fixity or recovery. The inference that is safe is structural. A fiber that leaves porosity and a weak bead interface is a poor permanent network, because the network fails before the amorphous phase has finished its cycle. Bouguermouh et al. (2026) supply the shape-memory counterpart for a natural fiber: flax raised initial modulus, hydrothermal aging damaged the interface, and fixity and recovery declined only moderately. Elongation is the other penalty, and it is documented more clearly for blend softening than for fiber loading. Rahmatabadi et al. (2024) needed 45 wt% polybutylene adipate terephthalate to reach 220% elongation. A highly aligned continuous fiber would be expected to cut that ductility, but the open shape-memory abstracts do not pair a measured failure strain with a carbon-fiber volume fraction in a programming test. The elongation penalty for carbon-fiber polylactic acid therefore remains a mechanical observation, not yet a shape-memory measurement, in this set.
