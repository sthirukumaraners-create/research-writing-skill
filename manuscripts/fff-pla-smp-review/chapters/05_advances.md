# 5. Fiber-Reinforced, Hybrid, and Multi-Material Routes

## 5.1 Blends that make polylactic acid programmable

Before the fiber is added, much of the shape-memory progress in the open set is a blend. Rahmatabadi et al. (2024) use 15%, 30%, and 45% polybutylene adipate terephthalate to soften polylactic acid without carbon fillers, explicitly as a response to the slow thermal response and the environmental cost of synthetic carbon. All three printed blends recovered, the 15% and 30% grades completely in under 8 s and the 45% grade to 93% in a few seconds with 220% elongation. Scanning electron microscopy showed printable layers in all three, with the least porosity and the best interlayer quality at 30%. Song et al. (2023) compatibilize polylactic acid/polycaprolactone at the amorphous regions and obtain 100% fixity and 94.5% shape-memory ratio after printing. Hamidi et al. (2026) and Fereydoonpour et al. (2025) map thermoplastic polyurethane from a toughening additive to a bi-material architecture, with the metric-dependent disagreement already noted. Doostmohammadi et al. (2024) print a 40/60 polylactic acid/poly(methyl methacrylate) blend on a pellet machine to 65.2 MPa, 17% above the polylactic acid they compare against, and 100% recovery, then demonstrate a bio-screw. Aljanabi et al. (2026) report mechanical, thermal, morphological, and shape-memory properties of polyethylene terephthalate glycol/polylactic acid blends; the open record available here did not include an abstract, so no ratio from that paper is used. Honari et al. (2025) change the internal structure instead of the chemistry, foaming polylactic acid with azodicarbonamide during pellet printing so that fixity and recovery can be traded through foam content and programming temperature.

The blend papers matter for fiber composites because they show how far matrix ductility can be moved before a yarn is added. A continuous fiber laid into a 17%-elongation matrix and a continuous fiber laid into a 220%-elongation matrix will not experience the same programming strain. Rahmatabadi et al. (2024) also show that the fastest recovery in their set came from the softest blend, aided by the low melting temperature of the adipate terephthalate phase. Carbon filler is not the only route to a faster response, which is the comparison they set out to make.

## 5.2 Natural fibers, silk, and cellulosic reinforcements

Natural fibers enter both as hygroscopic actuators and as stiffeners. de Kergariou et al. (2023) remain the clearest path-programmed flax/polylactic acid actuator, including a filament-scale finite-element model of the printed architectures. Mehaba et al. (2025) show that in-situ impregnation can vary continuous-flax weight fraction inside one print, and they deposited 0%, 30%, 40%, and 50% after choosing nozzle temperature and cooling. Quereilhac et al. (2025) attack the yarn architecture upstream of the nozzle: commingled and wrapped flax/polylactic acid yarns kept fibers parallel to the yarn axis, cut intra-yarn porosity by as much as 3.3 times relative to the less commingled route, and raised modulus by a factor of 2.1 relative to earlier twisted-yarn work. Jain et al. (2025) compare continuous regenerated-cellulose fibers in an in-situ infiltration extrusion. The higher-crystallinity Biomid fiber, about 65% crystalline, developed about 31% higher apparent interfacial shear strength than Cordenka at about 42% crystallinity. Interface strength is therefore a fiber-selection variable, not only a printer variable.

Bouguermouh et al. (2026) add the durability test the actuator papers still need. Flax in a polylactic acid/polyethylene terephthalate glycol 4D-printing composite raised modulus modestly and water uptake dramatically, and after 45 °C aging the mechanical losses were large while fixity stayed near 90% and recovery for the 15 wt% flax composite was highlighted near 97%. Bastida et al. (2026) explain why flax and hemp should not be pooled in a single “natural fiber” process model: flax favored interlayer fusion and a strength peak near 10 wt%, with impact strength doubling at 15 wt% through pull-out, whereas hemp hindered fusion and resisted environmental attack. Beg et al. (2024) show the stiffness ceiling of a short hemp-hurd reinforcement, 7.1 GPa at 40 wt%, with strength improved only at the lower loading. Hamat et al. (2025) show that a polydopamine coating on kenaf can recover adhesion in recycled polylactic acid, lifting ultimate tensile strength to 44.5 MPa against 18.6 MPa for uncoated recycled polylactic acid, with flexural strength highest at 5 wt% kenaf (54.7 MPa) and compression strength highest at 20 wt% (65.4 MPa). Selvamani et al. (2026) optimize New Zealand flax content jointly with raster angle and raster width. Shahar et al. (2022), Rezaieyan et al. (2024), Zarna et al. (2023), and Vigneshwaran et al. (2024) extend wood, cork, and kenaf polylactic acid into fatigue, acoustics, and sandwich bending. They establish that these fillers print and carry load. They do not report a shape-memory ratio.

Continuous silk is the bio-fiber route with an explicit recovery number beside a large strength gain. Rahmani et al. (2026) co-deposit silk with polylactic acid and bamboo charcoal from pellets, avoiding a separate filament stage. Yang et al. (2026) print continuous silk/polylactic acid with controlled ply orientation and content so that strength, modulus, and impact can be programmed by the stack. Yang et al. do not report fixity or recovery in the abstract. Rahmani et al. do: full recovery after 25% compression and 85% after 45%, with the unreinforced polymer sagging at 70 °C under load. Pellet-fed co-deposition and orientation-controlled silk are both manufacturing answers to the same bio-fiber opportunity. Only one of them has closed the shape-memory measurement.

Cellulose nanocrystals sit between a filler and a fiber. Umayanga et al. (2026) hold nanocrystal content fixed and tune polyethylene glycol, then print a gripper with about 93% fixity and 94% recovery near physiological temperature, retaining about 98% of that recovery ratio at cycle fifteen. Wan Ishak et al. (2025) use 0.75% nanocrystals in polylactic acid/liquid natural rubber to gain about 20% in degradation rate and to print a porous, cytocompatible scaffold. Karimipour et al. (2026) reinforce a printed dental implant with nanocrystals and coat it with polycaprolactone/nano-hardystonite electrospun fiber to change heat transfer and thermomechanical response. Golan et al. (2026) reinforce polylactic acid with 0.5 wt% tungsten disulfide nanotubes and examine cytocompatibility and hydrolytic degradation of the printed scaffold. These are biomedical process–structure papers. The gripper study is the one that also reports the shape-memory ratios.

## 5.3 Carbon fiber, carbon nanoparticles, and magnetic fillers

Short and recycled carbon fiber have a dense mechanical literature in this window and almost no shape-memory ratios. Saleh et al. (2024) gain up to 58.30% tensile modulus with desized recycled short carbon fiber and a mixing route that limited thermal alteration of the polylactic acid. Gajjar et al. (2026) deliver a full process window for short-carbon polylactic acid, including the 0° raster and 100% infill optimum already cited, and they frame interlayer bonding and defects as the reason a multi-response optimization is required. Bermudo Gamboa et al. (2023) find that the fiber does not improve rotational-bending fatigue. Vălean et al. (2024a, 2024b, 2025) find poorer notched and unnotched behavior than unfilled polylactic acid and interpret porosity and interlayer adhesion as the cause; the 2024a paper organizes notch-angle results with averaged strain energy density, and Arrieta et al. (2025) compare the theory of critical distances with averaged strain energy density for notched graphene-reinforced plates at a fixed 45/−45 raster. Both fracture papers are relevant to a programmed hinge, because a hinge is a notch that is asked to survive many folds. They are not recovery tests.

Continuous carbon is further along as a manufacturing problem and equally silent, in these abstracts, on fixity. Huang et al. (2026) optimize multi-nozzle impregnation of continuous-carbon/polylactic acid prepreg and measure unimpregnated area and nominal fiber volume fraction across 225–235 °C and 15.7–22.0 mm/s. Sankar et al. (2025) build polylactic acid/multi-wall carbon nanotube filaments, find tensile and thermal gains at 0.5 and 2 wt% nanotubes, and then print those filaments with continuous carbon fiber to evaluate electromagnetic shielding. Roy et al. (2026) impregnate continuous glass with graphene-modified polylactic acid on a dedicated line and filament-wind laminates, arguing that pre-impregnation beats co-extrusion for uniformity. The process knowledge required to put continuous carbon into polylactic acid without dry cores is accumulating. The programming experiment that would say whether that carbon raises recovery stress or cuts recoverable strain is not in the open 2022–2026 set reviewed here.

Nanoparticles are ahead of short carbon fiber on the functional side. Dantas et al. (2026) use carbon black for toughness, conductivity, electrically triggered recovery near 90%, and shielding up to 25 dB. Kumar et al. (2025) and Jeevan et al. (2026) raise tensile strength with carbon nanotubes, graphene, or 0.5 wt% graphene, the latter from 28.86 MPa to 41 MPa. Muhammad et al. (2025) predict up to an 11% gain in modulus and thermal conductivity at 2 wt% nanofiller from a multiscale model tied to printing. Angili et al. (2025) show that 0.6 wt% graphene oxide or nanotubes already agglomerate in printed scaffolds, so the reinforcement curve bends. Maleki et al. (2024) print alumina/graphene polylactic acid by digital light processing rather than fused filament; wear improved and graphene agglomerates cut tensile strength. That result should not be copied onto a raster-dependent extrusion part. Liu et al. (2022) remain the filament-level graphene study, at an optimum near 0.2 wt% for monofilament mechanics.

Magnetic fillers open a field-driven route. Moradi et al. (2023) magnetize iron-filled polylactic acid during fused deposition and couple field direction to bead direction. Kardos et al. (2026) compare three commercial magnetic polylactic acid filaments with neat polylactic acid. SteelFill showed the lowest flexural strength in their set, 34.1 MPa, and a tensile modulus of 19.2 MPa; none of the composites was cytotoxic in short contact or electrically conductive; SteelFill and an iron-filled grade deflected in a homogeneous magnetic field. The authors describe 4D-printing potential. They do not report a thermal fixity or recovery ratio. Magnetic deflection and thermal shape memory are both time-dependent functions. They need separate metrics.

## 5.4 Multi-material architectures and devices

Multi-material printing in this set is mostly polylactic acid against a softer polymer, not a continuous-fiber hinge bonded to an elastomer in one quantified recovery test. Fereydoonpour et al. (2025) print polylactic acid, polylactic acid/25% thermoplastic polyurethane, and polylactic acid/50% thermoplastic polyurethane as bi-material metamaterials and then fatigue them before recovery. Zhang et al. (2023) print a polylactic acid orthopedic plate and coat it with polycaprolactone/akermanite nanofiber of about 253 nm diameter to improve tissue-facing behavior. Karimipour et al. (2026) use a similar coating logic on a nanocrystal-reinforced implant. These are multi-material in the stack of surface and core. They are not the printed rigid–flexible hinge in which polylactic acid is the elastic reservoir and thermoplastic polyurethane is a living hinge with a reported recovery stress.

The devices that are actually demonstrated are still the best guide to function. Huang et al. (2025) deploy an origami-inspired structure on the optimized hinge in 8 s. Umayanga et al. (2026) print a thermoresponsive gripper at 40.5 °C. Doostmohammadi et al. (2024) print a bio-screw. de Kergariou et al. (2023) print a calla-lily-inspired hygromorph that did not reach the intended anticlastic curvature, and then they changed the path until curvature was controllable. Desole et al. (2026) cycle cellular springs. Honari et al. (2025) aim foamed polylactic acid at lightweight, insulating, shock-absorbing programmable parts. Rahmani et al. (2026) point honeycomb and trapezium meta-composites toward pallets, chassis inserts, and interior panels, with quasi-constant-force and quasi-zero-stiffness plateaus plus recovery. Vascular stents and self-folding biomedical tubes are discussed in the broader polylactic acid 4D-printing literature surveyed by Aghabarari et al. (2025). They are not among the devices measured in the open Elsevier articles read here. Claiming them as demonstrated fiber-reinforced fused-filament outcomes would outrun the evidence.
