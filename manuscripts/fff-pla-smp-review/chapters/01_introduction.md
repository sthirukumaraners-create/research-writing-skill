# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 From printed geometry to a shape that arrives later

Four-dimensional printing adds a programmed shape change to an additively manufactured object. The printed geometry is one configuration. A stimulus—heat, moisture, or an electric current that heats a conductive network—moves the part toward another configuration on a time scale set by the material and by the way it was deposited (Aghabarari et al., 2025; Muthe et al., 2022). The stimulus does not redraw the toolpath. It releases, or rebalances, deformation that the polymer and its reinforcements were able to hold. Polylactic acid has become a common carrier for that idea because it can be extruded as a filament or a pellet, it is bio-based, and its amorphous phase can be mobilized at temperatures that a laboratory oven, a heated fixture, or even a mildly warmed device can reach (Aghabarari et al., 2025; Muthe et al., 2022; Rahmatabadi et al., 2024).

Aghabarari et al. (2025) review this landscape broadly. They treat polylactic acid blends and composites together, covering thermal and mechanical properties, stimuli, manufacturing routes, and three molecular pictures of the shape-memory effect: a dual-state mechanism, a dual-component mechanism, and a partial-transition mechanism. They also place fibers, particles, and bioactive fillers inside a materials-selection argument aimed at biomedical, aerospace, automotive, and robotic uses. That breadth is useful, and it leaves a narrower manufacturing question open. Once the feedstock is fixed, the fused-filament toolpath still decides where material, porosity, and fiber direction sit. Those choices are process variables, and they are the subject of the present review.

## 1.2 Why polylactic acid is the default filament

Across the open studies, polylactic acid is the matrix that is actually printed, blended, foamed, or co-deposited with a continuous yarn (Honari et al., 2025; Muthe et al., 2022; Rahmani et al., 2026). Its practical advantages show up as printability and as a recoverable strain, not as a single universal glass-transition temperature. The programming and recovery temperatures that the studies actually use sit in a band from about 40 °C to about 85 °C, and the position inside that band depends on plasticizer, blend partner, and the chosen training condition. Umayanga et al. (2026) lowered the glass transition of a polylactic acid/cellulose nanocrystal composite from 49 °C to 40.5 °C by adding higher-molecular-weight polyethylene glycol, and they actuated a printed gripper at 40.5 °C. Hacıoğlu et al. (2026) found shape fixity rising from 90.2% to 98.8% as the programming temperature moved from 50 °C to 55 °C, then selected a Pareto point at 67.5 °C programming and 80 °C recovery. Huang et al. (2025) trained polylactic acid hinges at 65 °C for a first-cycle recovery of 99.53%, and they observed faster recovery, with a shorter life, when training sat between 75 °C and 85 °C, near the glass-transition region where over-recovery also appeared.

Unmodified printed polylactic acid is strong enough for a specimen and often too brittle, or too creep-prone when warm, for a large programmed strain. Robsan Abebe et al. (2026) measured tensile strengths of 33.48 MPa, 29.13 MPa, and 33.45 MPa for three commercial polylactic acid filaments, against an allowable tensile strength of 60 MPa cited for conventional polylactic acid, and they recorded a thermally driven recovery of 6 mm within 2 s. Rahmatabadi et al. (2024) state the processing problem directly: polylactic acid is widely used for 3D and 4D printing of shape-memory polymers, and it needs to be softened before large deformations are available. A 15 wt% polybutylene adipate terephthalate blend kept a strength of 40 MPa with 17% elongation, whereas 45 wt% of the same blend reached 220% elongation and 93% shape recovery in a few seconds. Rahmani et al. (2026) showed the warm-creep side of the same limitation. Under 70 °C and constant load, unreinforced polylactic acid beams sagged, while a bamboo-charcoal and continuous-silk composite held its geometry.

## 1.3 What fibers change, and what they do not automatically fix

Fibers are introduced for stiffness, strength, flame behavior, conductivity, or moisture-driven actuation (Muthe et al., 2022; Rahmani et al., 2026; Saleh et al., 2024). The gains are real when the fiber is continuous, well impregnated, or present at a loading the matrix can still wet. Saleh et al. (2024) raised the tensile modulus of fused-deposition polylactic acid by up to 58.30% with desized short recycled carbon fiber. Beg et al. (2024) reached a Young’s modulus of 7.1 GPa at 40 wt% hemp hurd microfiber, about twice neat polylactic acid, while tensile strength improved only at 20 wt%, and only by 8%. Kumar et al. (2025) moved ultimate tensile strength from 28 MPa for neat polylactic acid to 48 MPa with 1.5 wt% carbon nanotubes and 0.5 wt% graphene, and Young’s modulus from 2.6 GPa to 4 GPa. Bouguermouh et al. (2026) recorded a smaller stiffness step in a shape-memory grade: flax raised the modulus of a polylactic acid/polyethylene terephthalate glycol blend from 3.6 GPa to about 4.0 GPa at 15 wt%.

The same papers, and the short-carbon studies beside them, show the cost. Baharlou and Ma (2025) found higher tensile strength in neat polylactic acid than in short-carbon-fiber polylactic acid across unidirectional, cross-ply, and quasi-isotropic robotic layups. Vălean et al. (2024b, 2025) report poorer static and fatigue, or static and impact, behavior for short-carbon polylactic acid than for unfilled polylactic acid, and they point to porosity and weak adhesion between filament layers. Bermudo Gamboa et al. (2023) likewise found no fatigue benefit from short carbon fiber in rotational bending. Bastida et al. (2026) separate two natural fibers that are often treated as interchangeable: flax, smoother and lower in lignin, supported melt flow and interlayer adhesion, with a tensile optimum near 10 wt%, whereas hemp, rougher and richer in lignin, increased porosity and resisted degradation. A fiber can act as a permanent constraint on chain motion only if that interface survives programming strains and, for natural fibers, water. Bouguermouh et al. (2026) are explicit on the second point. The 15 wt% flax composite took up about 20% water after 67 days, against less than 2% for the neat blend, and aging at 45 °C cost up to 44% of stiffness and 69% of strength through swelling, debonding, and matrix fragmentation. Shape fixity and recovery largely remained. The interface did not.

## 1.4 Scope and the literature screen

This review asks how fused-filament process parameters and fiber architecture set the shape-memory response of polylactic acid composites. The question is narrower than the blend-and-application map in Aghabarari et al. (2025) and narrower than the bio-reinforcement survey of Muthe et al. (2022). Both of those reviews are used here as orientation. The evidence that follows is drawn from open-access Elsevier journal articles published from 2022 through 20 November 2026. Records were identified in Crossref for Elsevier, retained when a Creative Commons license was already in force and Unpaywall marked the work open, and kept when the title addressed polylactic acid shape-memory or 4D printing, or fiber-reinforced polylactic acid made by material extrusion. Abstracts were read from the publisher record, the Directory of Open Access Journals, or OpenAlex. One further paper, the Springer review of Aghabarari et al. (2025), is cited because it defines the broad field against which the process argument is set. Sixty-two Elsevier articles plus that review are cited. The screen was a relevance-ranked retrieval with a license filter, not a claim that every open ScienceDirect paper in the interval was captured. Where an abstract does not state a number, this review does not supply one.

The argument proceeds from the programming cycle and the metrics of fixity and recovery, to the fused-filament variables that the studies actually changed, to the tests used to see a recovery, to the fiber and multi-material routes, and then to anisotropy, interfaces, cycle life, and the absence of a shared macroscopic test. Shape-memory ratios and fiber mechanics are discussed as one chain only where a study measured both. Elsewhere they are kept distinct, because that distinction is what the open literature currently supports.
