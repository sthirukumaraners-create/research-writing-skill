# Abstract

Fused filament fabrication can store a temporary shape in polylactic acid and return the part toward its printed geometry when the amorphous phase is mobilized by heat, moisture, or electrical heating. Fiber reinforcement is often added to raise stiffness and to give the toolpath a structural memory, yet the same fibers change porosity, interlayer adhesion, and hydrothermal durability. This review examines that process–structure–property chain for fiber-reinforced polylactic acid. The primary literature is a screened set of 62 open-access Elsevier journal articles published from 2022 through 2026, read at title and abstract level, together with one 2025 review that maps polylactic acid 4D printing across blends and reinforcements. In the shape-memory studies, programming temperature, recovery temperature, blend ratio, and layer architecture move fixity and recovery by amounts that are large enough to matter for a device: fixity rises sharply between 50 °C and 55 °C in one programming study, an optimized hinge recovers 99.53% on the first cycle and about 25% after 50 cycles at 65 °C, and a cellulose-nanocrystal composite gripper fixes and recovers near 93–94% at about 40 °C. Blend ratio often shifts ductility and recovery more than a small change in print speed. Continuous flax, silk, and carbon routes raise stiffness or strength when impregnation and alignment are controlled, while several short-carbon-fiber studies report lower tensile, bending, or fatigue performance than unfilled polylactic acid and attribute the loss to porosity and weak interlayer bonding. The open set still separates these two literatures. Carbon-fiber toolpaths are well described mechanically, and shape-memory ratios are well described for neat and blended polylactic acid, but a single study rarely reports both the fiber architecture and the fixity, recovery, and cyclic force of a fused-filament part. The practical opportunity is a bio-based, printable actuator whose process window is now quantifiable for hinges, grippers, foams, and cellular lattices, provided interface durability and multi-cycle recovery are measured with a shared protocol.
