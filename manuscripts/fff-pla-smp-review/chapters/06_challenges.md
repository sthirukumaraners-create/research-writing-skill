# 6. Challenges and Future Perspectives

## 6.1 Predicting directional recovery

Anisotropy is already a measured fact for fused-filament polylactic acid and its carbon-fiber grades. Toprak et al. (2026) find build orientation more consequential for energy use than the choice between neat and carbon-fiber feedstock, and they find the on-edge orientation strongest in tension for both. Baharlou and Ma (2025) show that a quasi-isotropic raster repairs weak directions without making short-carbon polylactic acid stronger than the neat polymer. Gajjar et al. (2026) and Selvamani et al. (2026) arrive at different optimal rasters because one optimizes short-carbon tension and the other optimizes flax-filled flexure. de Kergariou et al. (2023) go further for actuation: curvature is a function of path and of the angle given to passive flax layers, and a filament-scale finite-element model is offered to capture that architecture. Hacıoğlu et al. (2026) and Doostmohammadi et al. (2024) show that surrogates and neural nets can interpolate programming conditions or pellet-printing windows once the experiments exist. Arrieta et al. (2025) and Vălean et al. (2024a) show that notch-failure loads in printed reinforced plates can be estimated with critical-distance and strain-energy methods at a known raster.

What is still missing is a model that takes a fiber-reinforced toolpath and returns fixity, recovery, and recovery force after a stated number of cycles. The hygromorph model predicts curvature under humidity (de Kergariou et al., 2023). The programming surrogates predict ratios for a polylactic acid coupon whose raster is not the object of the model (Hacıoğlu et al., 2026). The fracture models predict load at a notch, not the strain returned on reheating (Arrieta et al., 2025). Until those pieces are joined, directional recovery of a carbon-fiber or flax-fiber hinge will be found by printing the hinge. That is acceptable for a device study. It is a poor substitute for a process map.

## 6.2 Interfaces that must survive the programming strain

The mechanical papers already name the interface failure that a programming cycle would exploit. Vălean et al. (2024b, 2025) attribute poorer static, fatigue, bending, and impact results of short-carbon polylactic acid to porosity and weak adhesion between layers. Bermudo Gamboa et al. (2023) see no fatigue gain from the same class of fiber once layer thickness, temperature, and speed are in play. Bastida et al. (2026) show hemp disrupting interlayer fusion through roughness and lignin, while flax improves it until aggregation sets in. Jain et al. (2025) quantify the fiber side of the interface: a 23-percentage-point crystallinity difference between regenerated-cellulose fibers corresponded to about 31% difference in apparent interfacial shear strength. Huang et al. (2026) quantify the dry-core side for continuous carbon, using unimpregnated area as a process metric. Hamat et al. (2025) show that a coating can move a natural-fiber interface enough to change tensile, flexural, and compressive rankings with fiber content. Bourgogne et al. (2026) show that the interface and the matrix are already altered before printing, because extruding the reinforced filament to 0.4 mm can cut modulus by up to half.

Bouguermouh et al. (2026) are the study that watches this interface inside a shape-memory experiment. Hot-water aging produced fiber swelling, debonding, matrix fragmentation, porosity, a stronger hydroxyl signal, and secondary recrystallization of polylactic acid. Strength losses reached 69% and modulus losses 44%. Fixity and recovery declined much less than the quasi-static properties. The functional memory can outlast the structural margin. That is encouraging for a part that must move, and it is a warning for a part that must move and still carry load. A programming strain large enough to need the 220% elongation of a soft blend (Rahmatabadi et al., 2024) will interrogate bead interfaces and fiber pull-out every cycle. The open set does not yet report interfacial shear strength before and after a carbon-fiber polylactic acid programming series. That measurement is the logical next experiment, and the impregnation metrics of Huang et al. (2026) and the shear-strength comparison of Jain et al. (2025) are the baselines it should use.

## 6.3 Cycle life beyond the first return

First-cycle recovery near 100% is common in the abstracts (Bouguermouh et al., 2026; Doostmohammadi et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Song et al., 2023). Multi-cycle recovery is not. Huang et al. (2025) provide the sharpest decay: about 25% recovery after 50 cycles at 65 °C, with faster training temperatures shortening life further, and an origami deployment demonstrated on the young hinge. Umayanga et al. (2026) are more durable in a softer, plasticized nanocrystal composite, keeping about 98% of the original recovery ratio after fifteen cycles at low activation temperature. Desole et al. (2026) stop at three cycles and already record force drops of roughly 40% in the stiffer lattice. Fereydoonpour et al. (2025) show that fatigue loading changes the subsequent memory response more than a monotonic preload, and that layered specimens delaminate if the test frequency meets a structural resonance. Honari et al. (2025) trade fixity against recovery with foam content and programming temperature, which implies that a foam tuned for a perfect first lock may be the wrong foam for repeated release.

A review of actuators cannot treat a single recovery as a material property. The minimum report that would make studies comparable is first-cycle fixity and recovery, the same ratios at a declared later cycle, and the temperature of both programming and recovery. Huang et al. (2025) and Umayanga et al. (2026) meet that bar and disagree with each other in a physically plausible way: a thin polylactic acid hinge trained at 65 °C wears out, and a plasticized composite actuated near 40 °C holds its ratio longer. Fiber-reinforced grades still lack this pair of numbers.

## 6.4 A shared test, and the boundary of the evidence

Section 4 recorded the diversity of folds, compression recoveries, fatigue-then-recover sequences, humid curling, hot air, and hot water. That diversity is a methods problem, not a literary one. An ASTM or ISO method for the macroscopic shape-memory effect of a fiber-reinforced printed polymer would specify the stimulus, the programmed geometry, the fiber angle relative to the strained ligament, the cycle count, and the difference between free recovery and blocked force. None of the open abstracts states that such a method was followed. Mechanical and biological standards are already used beside it (Vălean et al., 2025; Wan Ishak et al., 2025). They do not replace it.

Environmental accounting belongs in the same future test plan, because fiber choice is partly an environmental choice. Enarevba et al. (2025) compare hemp-straw, hemp-hurd, and hemp-fiber filaments blended with polylactic acid and find the straw-filled filament lowest in endpoint impacts, with neat polylactic acid highest in global-warming potential and the fiber-filled filament higher in cumulative energy demand because of density. Enarevba et al. (2026) take hemp-straw powder into printed parts under a gate-to-gate assessment. Desole et al. (2026) show that local heating, by shrinking the heated volume about 14%, changes the human-health indicator of a lattice recovery cycle, with the sign of the trade-off depending on the energy efficiency of the heater. A faster recovery that needs an 80 °C chamber (Hacıoğlu et al., 2026) and a slower recovery at 40 °C (Umayanga et al., 2026) are not environmentally equivalent, even when both ratios look excellent on cycle one.

The boundary of this review should stay visible. It covers open-access Elsevier journal articles from 2022 through 2026 that the license and title screen retained, plus the broad 2025 synthesis of Aghabarari et al. (2025). Paywalled fused-filament studies of carbon-fiber shape-memory polylactic acid may exist outside that screen. They are not used here. Within the screen, the process–structure map for short and continuous carbon fiber is comparatively rich, and the shape-memory map for neat, blended, foamed, plasticized, flax, silk, and carbon-black polylactic acid is comparatively rich. The intersection is thin. That thin intersection is the research gap, and it is a gap in published open measurements rather than a gap in plausible mechanisms.
